The Tiny Language Model Lab Notebook

Chapter 1. A small beginning.

Every morning the researcher opened the notebook and wrote one sentence.
The sentence was never very long, but it was always honest.
Today we train a small model, she wrote, and we will train it on the CPU.
The machine was old and quiet, and it had no fancy graphics card.
It had a fan that hummed softly and a memory that was small but clean.
The researcher liked the quiet machine because it never complained.
It simply computed, one batch at a time, until the numbers improved.

A language model, she reminded herself, is only a probability machine.
It looks at the tokens it has seen and guesses what should come next.
If the guess is good, the loss goes down and the weights are kept.
If the guess is bad, the loss goes up and the weights are nudged.
This is the whole idea, repeated millions of times, patiently.
There is no magic in the loop, only arithmetic and a little patience.

Chapter 2. Tokens and characters.

A tokenizer turns text into numbers that a computer can work with.
In this little lab we use the simplest tokenizer of all: characters.
Every letter, space, comma and newline becomes an integer id.
The model does not know the meaning of a single character.
It only knows that some characters tend to follow other characters.
After the letter t, perhaps the letter h will appear.
After a newline, perhaps the word The will appear again.
This is enough to start, and starting is the hardest part.

The vocabulary is the set of all characters in the corpus.
We sort them and give each one a number from zero upward.
When the model speaks, it produces numbers, and we turn them back into text.
The circle is closed: text to numbers, numbers to text.
The researcher drew this circle in the margin of her notebook.
She drew it again whenever she felt confused, which was often.

Chapter 3. The shape of the model.

The model is a stack of transformer blocks, one after another.
Each block has two jobs: mix information across positions, and think.
The mixing job is called attention, and it is surprisingly simple.
Each token looks back at earlier tokens and decides what to pay attention to.
It cannot look forward, because the future has not happened yet.
This rule is called causality, and it keeps the model honest.
A mask hides the future so that cheating is impossible.

The thinking job is a small neural network applied to each position.
It takes a vector, expands it, applies a smooth function, and shrinks it back.
Between these jobs there are normalisations that keep the numbers calm.
And around each job there is a shortcut, a residual path, so gradients can flow.
Stack a few blocks together and you have a tiny GPT.
It is much smaller than the famous models, but it is made of the same ideas.

Chapter 4. Training on the CPU.

The researcher set the device to cpu and checked the settings.
The batch size was sixteen and the context length was one hundred twenty eight.
The learning rate was tiny, three times ten to the minus four.
Three layers, four heads, and an embedding width of one hundred twenty eight.
These numbers were not special; they were simply small enough to be friendly.
On a GPU they would have been laughable, but on the CPU they were perfect.

The training loop was short enough to fit on one page.
Take a batch, compute the loss, clear the gradients, backpropagate, step.
Clip the gradients so that no single update becomes too large.
Warm up the learning rate at the start, then decay it like a cosine.
Print the loss every ten steps so that progress can be seen.
Every few hundred steps, let the model write a sample.
The sample is usually nonsense at first, and then slowly becomes less strange.

Chapter 5. Watching the loss.

At step zero the loss was about four point one.
The model was guessing almost uniformly among the characters.
By step one hundred the loss had fallen below three.
The model had learned that spaces are common and that the letter e is frequent.
By step five hundred the loss was near two.
The model had learned the shape of words and the rhythm of sentences.
The researcher smiled at the numbers because they told a simple story.
Learning was happening, quietly, on a machine with no graphics card.

Sometimes the loss on the validation split was higher than on training.
This was expected, because the model had never seen those characters in that order.
The gap was a reminder that memorising is not the same as understanding.
The researcher wrote this down, because she wanted to remember it later.

Chapter 6. The first sample.

The model was given a single token and asked to continue.
At first it produced strings like: the the the and and of of to to.
Then it produced words that looked almost real: tha, wich, lern, modle.
Then one morning it wrote: the small model learns one token at a time.
The researcher read the line twice and laughed out loud.
It was not profound, but it was grammatical, and it was new.
No one had written that sentence before, at least not in that corpus.
The machine had arranged old characters into a fresh pattern.
This, she thought, is the essence of generation.

Chapter 7. Sampling and temperature.

A model outputs a probability for every character in the vocabulary.
Greedy sampling always picks the most likely one, which is safe but dull.
Temperature changes the sharpness of the distribution.
A low temperature makes the model cautious and repetitive.
A high temperature makes it wild and often wrong.
Top-k sampling throws away all but the k most likely candidates.
These knobs let a person steer the model without retraining it.
The researcher kept a temperature of zero point eight in her notes.
It was a good middle ground between boring and chaotic.

Chapter 8. What the model cannot do.

A tiny character model has no world knowledge and no long memory.
It cannot reason about facts that were not in its training text.
It forgets things that happened more than a few hundred characters ago.
It confuses names and repeats phrases when it is unsure.
These limitations are not bugs; they are consequences of scale.
Give the model more parameters and more data and the picture changes.
But the principles stay exactly the same: predict the next token, and repeat.

The researcher wrote a warning at the bottom of the page.
A fluent sentence is not proof of understanding.
The model can sound certain while being completely wrong.
Trust the evaluation, not the tone of voice.
She underlined this twice, because it was important.

Chapter 9. Saving the work.

When the run finished, the researcher saved a checkpoint.
It contained the weights, the vocabulary, and the settings of the model.
A checkpoint is a snapshot of a moment in training.
It lets you stop today and continue the story tomorrow.
She could also load it and simply ask the model to write more text.
This was the part she liked best: the model was now a tool.

The fan on the old machine kept humming after the loop ended.
The loss curve was drawn on a page and taped to the wall.
It went down quickly at first and then more slowly, as curves do.
There was still noise, but the direction was clear.
Progress, she wrote, is mostly a matter of showing up every step.

Chapter 10. The next experiment.

There were many ideas left to try.
A larger context window would let the model remember more.
More layers would give it more room to think.
A bigger corpus would teach it more words and more facts.
A better tokenizer could split text into pieces smaller than words but larger than letters.
Each idea would need its own run and its own page in the notebook.
The researcher closed the notebook and looked at the quiet machine.
Tomorrow they would train again, one batch at a time.
The little model had learned to speak, and the lab had learned to listen.
